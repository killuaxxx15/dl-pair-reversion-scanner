1.What this tool looks for

Markets stretch relationships between assets, then snap them back. Gold runs far ahead of stocks, semis run far ahead of the Nasdaq — and eventually the relationship reverts toward its long-run average. This scanner watches a list of such pairs and tells us when a stretch has become extreme and has started to reverse. Both conditions matter. The first without the second is a falling knife.

2.The ratio

Every pair is just one price divided by another. GLD/SPY means: how many units of the S&P does one unit of gold buy? When the ratio rises, gold is winning. We trade the relationship, not either asset outright — long one leg, short the other — so the position is roughly insulated from the overall market going up or down. Prices are dividend- and split-adjusted, so a high yielder is not made to look cheap by its own distributions.

3.Is the stretch extreme? (the setup)

We compare today's ratio to its own 5-year average and ask how unusual it is, measured in standard deviations — the z-score. A z of zero means the relationship is at its normal level. A z of +3 means it is stretched further than almost all of its own history. Our threshold is 3 sigma: below that, the stretch is noise; at or beyond it, it is a candidate.

A caution on that number. In a textbook bell curve a 3-sigma reading happens about once in 370 observations. Asset ratios have fatter tails than the bell curve, so in practice 3-sigma readings arrive rather more often than the statistics imply — and they overshoot to 4 and 5 sigma more often too. Treat 3 sigma as a screening threshold, not as a probability.

One subtlety: we use the peak z of the recent past, not just today's. By the time a reversal is confirmed, the ratio has usually already fallen and today's z looks tame. What matters is that the relationship was at an extreme recently — that is the stored tension the trade harvests. The lookback is deliberately asymmetric: one year for stretches to the upside, two years for stretches to the downside. See section 9.

4.Has it started to turn? (the trigger)

An extreme reading alone is not a trade. Things that are 3 sigma expensive routinely go to 4 and 5 sigma. So we wait for the trend itself to crack, measured three ways against the 50-, 100- and 200-day averages. Each one the ratio has crossed in the direction of the reversion scores one point — those are the three pips in the scanner. We want at least 2 of 3, plus one more confirmation: the 50-day average itself must have turned, not just been pierced on a bad week (we measure its change over the past 20 days).

The test mirrors by side, which matters when reading the pips. On a short idea — a ratio stretched too high — a point is scored for each average the ratio has fallen below, and the 50-day must be sloping down. On a long idea — a ratio washed out too low — it is the exact opposite: a point for each average the ratio has climbed back above, and the 50-day must be sloping up. Three pips always means the same thing: the trend has broken in our favour.

The one-line version: the rubber band must be stretched to 3 sigma, and it must have visibly started to snap back, before we touch it.

5.The four buckets

HIGH CONVICTIONReached ≥3σ within the lookback, trend has broken (2 or 3 averages crossed in our favour, 50-day turned), and the ratio is still more than 10% away from its 5-year average. This is the actionable list.
WATCHLISTReached ≥3σ, but the trigger has not fully fired. Two ways in: the trend is still intact (0–1 averages crossed), or the averages have gone but the 50-day has not yet turned — the row note says slope not confirmed for the second. We do not fight either. We wait, and it graduates.
LARGELY DONEReached ≥3σ and the ratio now sits within 10% of its 5-year average. The distance left is not worth the risk, so we stand aside. Note this outranks the others: a pair that has round-tripped lands here even if its moving-average structure still looks like a fresh signal.
NO TRADENever reached 3σ in the lookback — nothing to do. A pair without enough price history to compute a 5-year statistic also lands here, so a brand-new ETF will read NO TRADE rather than announcing itself as unmeasurable.

Two notes on that "10%". It is the plain distance from today's ratio to its 5-year average — not 10% of the journey already travelled. And it is a flat number applied to every pair, so it is a tighter filter on a placid pair like XLU/SPY than on a violent one like COPX/GDX.

6.Targets and the exit

Target 1 is the ratio's own 200-day average — often reached quickly, sometimes on the very day the signal confirms. Target 2 is the full trip back to the 5-year mean.

The stop is mechanical and, like the trigger, it mirrors by side. On a short idea we are out if the ratio climbs back above its 50-day average and makes a new 20-day high. On a long idea we are out if it falls back below the 50-day and makes a new 20-day low. Both conditions must hold — one alone is noise. The exact level for each pair is printed on its expanded row. No averaging down, no thesis rescue.

7.Base rate — does this actually work?

For each pair, the scanner counts every resolved past episode in the data where the same conditions fired, and reports how often the ratio went on to reach its 200-day average within a year, and how long it took. A pair showing 3/4 · med 41d has delivered three times out of four, in a median of 41 trading days. Episodes still open at the end of the data are excluded rather than counted as failures. When the base rate is poor, that is the tool telling us the old average may no longer be the destination.

Two exclusions keep the number honest. An episode is only counted from the day after it triggers, so nothing gets credit for a move that had already happened. And an episode whose target was already met on the trigger day is discarded entirely rather than booked as an instant win — a ratio that gaps through its 50-day and 200-day averages in a single move has nothing left to predict. You will see the discarded count in the column, e.g. 4/6 … (5 discarded: target already met at trigger). Both exclusions shrink the sample; that is the point. The alternative is a hit rate that flatters itself.

8.What the tool does not know

Sometimes a relationship is not stretched — it has permanently changed. Central banks buying gold, AI capex rewriting semiconductor economics, a rate regime shift. The statistics cannot tell the difference between a rubber band and a broken one. That judgment stays with us. The rule of thumb: if the 5-year average itself is drifting steadily in one direction — visible as a sloping gold line on the pair chart — treat the raw sigma count with suspicion.

The scanner now measures that suspicion rather than leaving it to the eye. Alongside the ordinary z-score it computes a second one against the pair's own five-year trend line instead of its flat average. Where the two agree, the stretch is real dislocation. Where they disagree by more than a sigma the row is flagged z DIVERGES, and both numbers are shown on the expanded row. It cuts both ways: a raw reading that is the more extreme of the two means part of the apparent stretch is just drift, and the setup is weaker than it looks; a trend-adjusted reading that is the more extreme means the pair has moved a long way from its own path while the flat average quietly absorbed it. The tiers are still driven by the raw number — this is a second opinion, not a replacement.

One case the tool now refuses outright. If a ratio has been stretched three sigma in both directions inside the lookback, it is flagged WHIPSAW and dropped to NO TRADE regardless of how the rest of the setup reads. A pair that has done both is not oscillating around a stable average; it is telling us the average itself is moving, and every target we would derive from it — the 200-day, the mean, the invalidation level — inherits that instability. There is no side of that trade worth taking.

9.The long side: bases, not bounces

The buy side of this book — pairs stretched 3 sigma below their average and now turning up — matters more to us than the shorts, and it behaves differently. Blowoff tops resolve fast: the break comes and the reversion runs in weeks. Washed-out bottoms usually don't V-bottom; they base — twelve to eighteen months of sideways drift at the lows while sellers exhaust. Because of that, the tool remembers downside extremes for two years (versus one for tops), so a washout is not forgotten halfway through the base it created.

Every long candidate also carries a BASE score out of 4, one point each for: meaningful time spent at the lows, volatility drying up, the 200-day average flattening out, and a higher low versus the past year. The score maps directly to size: 3 or 4 — size normally, a durable turn and the trades we want to own; 2 — half size; 0 or 1 — bounce risk, half size or pass. The label on the signal says which. Two out of four is the most common reading in practice, so it gets an explicit instruction rather than being left to the analyst to interpret a number the tool declined to judge. One more nuance: after a crash, the rolling average gets dragged down toward the price, so the z-score can look tame while the ratio still sits 25% below its mean — which is why "largely done" is measured as distance remaining to the mean, not in sigma.
